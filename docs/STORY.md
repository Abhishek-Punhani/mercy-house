# Mercy House: The Ninth Burial

Original working fiction for a simulated investigation. Author edition, seed 7. All people and places are fictional. This manuscript is not a claim that no similar plot exists.

## I. The second copy

The last coach reached Bhairav’s Hollow before the bridge closed. Above the houses, Mercy House showed one lit window. Below them, the gravedigger’s garden had been roped off with washing line.

The detective carried a letter from Mira Sen. “Your sister’s statement was copied twice. You have only read the second copy. Come before the ground freezes.”

Tara had died twelve winters earlier. Raghav Sethi’s name had survived in every version of her statement. The detective had built a life around that name.

Mira was dead now. Dev Malik had blood on his sleeve. Three people said they had heard him threaten her before the ninth bell. Kabir, who had once followed the detective into a flooded cellar without hesitation, said there was no reason to make this difficult.

Anya asked who had heard the threat first.

Nobody liked her question.

## II. The garden

Two skeletons lay above the bulbs Dev had planted a month earlier. The cloth around them bore the laundry mark of the ninth ward. Their burial in the garden was recent; their deaths were not.

Dev admitted opening graves. Families paid him to retrieve rings and small possessions from graves without names. He had not moved these bodies. He had gone to Mira’s room after hearing a cry and pressed his sleeve to her wound.

“I ran because you would see the blood before you heard me,” he said.

In Savitri Das’s kitchen, the detective was offered tea. Savitri had made the same tea after Tara’s funeral. She remembered where the detective used to sit. She said Dev had always frightened her.

Beside her stove sat an ash bucket containing part of an unfinished letter.

## III. Those we trust

Professor Vedant Rao had designed the speaking tubes at Mercy House. His wheelchair had followed the same corridors for years; he knew which doors could be held open and which shutters carried sound.

He called the ninth ward an acoustic curiosity. The bell without a rope was, he said, an engineering problem.

Leena, Savitri’s daughter and the detective’s former partner, refused to discuss her correspondence with Mira. The letters were intimate. Their secrecy concerned an affair and access to restricted records, not a murder agreement. Privacy made her evasive; the detective’s memories made that evasiveness feel like guilt.

Kabir’s lie was different. Anya had inventoried an envelope before his watch. After it, the envelope was gone. Inside was a carrier’s appeal: Nalin, Kabir’s brother, had delivered the grave crates at Savitri’s instruction. Kabir removed the letter to protect him.

Neither revelation proved that Leena or Kabir had killed Mira.

## IV. The order and the omission

Raghav had ordered Tara’s confinement. The hospital duplicate carried his signature. He deserved the detective’s scrutiny.

But the original statement named Savitri too. Tara had asked her to open the door. Savitri had refused. The copybook showed which lines Savitri later ordered omitted.

Mira had been preparing a disclosure, but not a complete one. Her own earlier silence would have remained outside the story she intended to tell. In this first simulator version, that aspect of Mira’s past remains narrative context rather than a scored finding.

The detective could preserve the old hatred by reading only the circulated copy. The original required a more painful account.

## V. The ninth ward

Ishan touched a bloodstained sleeve and heard “Stay with me.” He could not name the speaker. He could not tell whether the words preceded the injury or followed it.

The cook could. She had hidden after watching Savitri strike Mira at 21:12 while Vedant held the door. Dev arrived afterward and spoke those words as he tried to help.

In the ninth ward, a sealed object repeated a phrase concealed from the observers. An inert replacement did not. Within this fictional world, the phenomenon was real and repeatable.

It was not omnipotent. Savitri and Vedant’s private exchange described the voice and their ability to refuse it. Their earlier refusals were written beside their later decision to act.

The entity was associated with Nera, the patient omitted from Vedant’s register. Its ultimate identity and motives were not established merely because it claimed a name. No honest report could turn a bounded observation into knowledge of everything haunting the house.

## VI. What happened

In seed 7, Savitri killed Mira; Vedant knowingly assisted. A planning letter, retained draft, direct observation and independent corridor records establish distinct parts of their responsibility.

Savitri forged Dev’s threat. Her draft, stationery and writing survive. She commissioned Nalin to move the bodies into Dev’s garden. Invoice duplicates, soil comparison and the cemetery graves connect the relocation.

Vedant arranged guide Harun’s killing after Harun saw the crates. The inquest, a retained threat and payment to the attacker establish a human cover-up. Elsie’s later death, though absorbed into the town’s rumours, followed her documented illness. It was not another act of this conspiracy.

The detective’s final difficulty was not naming a monster. It was describing several people accurately: an enemy guilty of an old wrong but not this murder; a friend who betrayed the investigation but did not kill; a former partner entitled to privacy but accountable for her actions; a comforting mother who chose violence under an influence she could resist.

The report ended with what the detective could prove. It left the rest open.

## Simulation variations

Even-numbered seeds reverse which member of the Savitri–Vedant pair inflicts the fatal injury and which knowingly assists. The generated documents and verifier change together. The historical concealment, garden framing and secondary deaths remain fixed in this baseline. Additional plot families are future work, not generated by this version.
